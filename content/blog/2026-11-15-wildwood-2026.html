I was chatting with someone today about how maybe the risk in taking fifteen years to make a movie is that your screenplay gets a little musty, and maybe that's just the trade-off you make with stop motion, so I shouldn't have been as disappointed with Wildwood as I was.🦊

After all, many people roundly loved it (it currently has 4.4 stars on letterboxd,) but it's interesting to read the more ambivalent reviews that are like "So beautiful! So much effort! A triumph of human artistry! The plot is a predictable string of tropes, but I'm giving it five stars because fuck AI!" in a way that's a little dissonant to me. What's the point of all this artistry and human taste if it didn't produce something you love?🗳️

What if it didn't take one hundred million dollars and fifteen years to make a movie? Would they have been able to take even more creative risks? That's why novels are great. I can just write "a hundred fire breathing dragons erupted out of the volcano" and no VFX supervisor has to wince and say we won't be able to depict that on budget.📕

But fewer people are reading novels and making movies is expensive. Artifical intelligence is nipping at the second fact, but if/when video-generating models get good enough to allow people to make visually impressive and coherent films for $10, how will they get curated? Can the phenomenon of Wildwood (2026) be separated from the fact that making it was expensive and very risky?

Not to mention that creative constraints are a good thing! It's good that directors can't trivially render one hundred dragons erupting out of a volcano. And countless beloved aesthetic developments have come from limitations of the medium: slapstick and silent film; speed stripes and anime; fog in silent hill. Current AI video generation is very constrained, but mostly in a bad way🐣 and attempts to overcome this aren't innately cherishable. We don't love it when cheap landlords paint over the mould in the bathroom either.

I don't know where this is going. I'm not anti-AI in a way that makes me love a film simply because they took the hard path, but I also recognize that human intent is currently still a necessary ingredient for meaning, and that expensive, messy, striving monuments are rare — valuable if for no other reason than a lot of people will go to see them and talk about them together afterwards, building shared metaphors, ways of collectively understanding ourselves, in-jokes, et cetera. Even if I was let down by Wildwood's plot, it has immense heart. I really am proud of all the artists and film industry people who toiled to make it happen.

Many people outright reject the possibility that something produced in large part "by AI" could ever be considered art, or good, or moving. Given that the current most famous aesthetic output of generative AI video today is Brain Rot, I see where they're coming from. But given how literally none of this was possible ten years ago, I also see where we're going, and I hold on to some optimism that we'll find ways to solve the "meaninglessness problem." We're destined for the holodeck, and I'm pretty sure trekkies don't watch this scene and think "Fucking slop. This is a terrible future, actually."
