--- title: on On unplugging it ---

A response to my good internet friend’s post about Nate Soares and AI existential risk. She is skeptical. I’m going to pull out the points that I either disagree with or don’t understand. Brevity not guaranteed.

What is intelligence?

“intelligence” is, mostly if not entirely, domain-specific. Getting better at math only indirectly helps me get better at reading, if it does at all. Domain-specific understanding doesn’t necessarily transfer.

Intelligence, the psychological construct, is the thing you have if you can do spatial reasoning, learn things quickly, solve novel problems, etc. You can measure it and it appears to correlate with many things. Admittedly, I can’t navigate via Earth’s magnetic field, but I am more intelligent than a pigeon. Magnus Carlsen♟️ could likely be a better writer or programmer than me if he wanted to be.

This is because while there are some ways in which skills transfer - learning formal logic probably sharpens your philosophy; learning Spanish improves your French🌤️ - it’s not the case that learning one domain will arbitrarily improve your capacity in other domains. Instead it seems that we (and LLMs) have a deeper, more general learning ability. If I can learn maths quickly, I am more likely to be able to learn history quickly, too. So someone who is “good at maths” is more likely on average to be “good at history”, but it's not that maths teaches them history.

[LLMs] perform better with (a) highly-structured languages such as programming languages and formalized math, and (b) in instances where output can be checked for binaristic correctness. I’ve talked about this vis-a-vis computer programming but I think the capacity to compile and run code is key to the fact that coding has gotten so effective.

Yup.📏 After teaching LLMs to predict the internet, teaching them to solve maths and coding problems appears to have helped bootstrap the jump in reasoning capabilites that we saw in 2025, and as models got better at maths and coding, they also got better at harder-to-check things. This implies the development of skills underneath - reasoning, persistence and creative problem-solving - that are similarly general purpose. Maths just gets cited a lot because it's easy to benchmark, not because it's the only thing improving.

Claude puts it this way: Progress is broad but uneven. Models improve most where we can train and check them directly, and less, but still noticeably, everywhere else.

I think it’s due to this that there’s been such a large divide between programmers and non-programmers on whether models are smart and getting smarter, because, for programmers, the progress has been so fast and easy to witness. Our job was (🥲) mostly doing coding benchmark tasks. Meanwhile, non-programmers with their fuzzier tasks, locked-down software, and physical world interactions have had fewer opportunities to substitute LLMs into their daily work. When they have, it’s been on tasks that haven't progressed as quickly as coding, so they’ve seen less of it, and in smaller jumps. From my side, this is a dynamic underlying a lot of the dissonance between AI boosters and skeptics.🦮

Recursive self-improvement

The two pieces of evidence they give: “internal benchmarks” and the fact that that they are pushing 45x as much code per engineer per day as they used to … I do not understand why we would believe a stateless, context-limited technology would be able to improve itself through that kind of scaling. LLM-assisted codebases sprawl, and this sprawl is not evidence of growing strength, but of an increasing number of failure points.

An increase in engineer output alone would not be bulletproof evidence for RSI, but it is just one piece among many others. Airplanes also have many more failure points than they did 120 years ago.

The way that models get better is: you find a new dataset or improve/refine an existing one; or you find a new machine-learning technique that learns more efficiently; or you find ways to more efficiently use energy.

Specifically, here’s a short recipe (written by Claude) for how a machine learning researcher might make an LLM better.
  1. Find a weakness. Look at where the model fails, using benchmark scores, user feedback, or reading lots of its outputs. For example: “It gives up too early on long multi-step tasks.”
  2. Form a hypothesis. Guess why it fails and what would fix it. For example: “It rarely saw examples of recovering from mistakes, so training on those should help.” The fix might be new data, a different training method, or a change to the reward signal.
  3. Build an evaluation. Before changing anything, make a test that measures the weakness, and record the current model’s score as a baseline. Keep the test data separate from training data, or the results mean nothing.
  4. Curate or generate data. Collect, filter and clean examples. Depending on the method, that could be web text, human-written demonstrations, human ratings of which answer is better, synthetic examples written by other models, or practice tasks with automatic grading for reinforcement learning.
  5. Design the training experiment. Choose the method: changing the pretraining data mix, fine-tuning on examples, or reinforcement learning against a reward. Set up a control run without the change so you can compare.
  6. Run it small first. Train cheap, small-scale versions to see whether the idea works at all, and whether the gain grows or disappears as models get bigger.
  7. Evaluate broadly. Check the target eval, plus a wide set of other tests to catch regressions, like the model getting worse at something else. Also check for cheating, where the model games the reward or the test instead of actually improving.
  8. Analyse why it worked (or didn’t). Read the outputs, and run ablations: remove pieces of the change one at a time to see which part caused the improvement. This often produces the next hypothesis, which takes you back to step 2.
  9. Scale up and fold it in. If the gain holds up, include the change in the next big training run, run safety testing, and ship.
  10. Monitor after release. Real-world use surfaces new weaknesses, which feed back into step 1.

AI models that can reason and write code and persist through long-timescale, novel problems are starting to be able to do all these things. The techniques that the companies have employed to teach their models how to solve millennium prize problems will also work well for machine learning research because machine learning research happens entirely in the digital world and much of it is verifiable.🤏 Dwarkesh Patel lays it out well.

And yes, this increases sprawl and the complexity of the training process, but AI companies aren’t random vibe-coded apps. They are devoting billions of dollars in salaries and inference to solve these problems. Hallucination might not be fundamentally solvable, but it’s also not machine learning’s Achilles’ heel, because of verifiability. It’s why AI can do superhuman mathematics and coding now, and it’s why they’ll be able to write experiments that make smarter AIs.

Transformers aren’t chess engines

…the goals of these projects remain tightly domain-limited. A simple algorithm run through high-powered software worked better for chess than more complex algorithms did, but the Deep Blue supercomputer that beat Garry Kasparov wasn’t good at other stuff just because it was good at chess. Within the domain of a problem, scale worked; the problem of “being as good at thinking as a person” is too general.

Sutton’s point is that general methods like search and learning are ultimately the most effective. Search-based engines beat hand-built chess knowledge; then AlphaZero, a more general learning algorithm, beat the best search-based engine while mastering shogi and Go too.

GPT-style models take this generalization even further. All they do is “predict the next token” and yet they can translate, write code, do arithmetic and pick up tasks from a few examples. They do this better than anyone else’s attempt to do the same with handcrafted rules, and scaling them up improves their capabilities at everything.

So I think it’s completely up for debate whether or not “being as good at thinking as a person” is “too general.” Beyond proving the Riemann Hypothesis, here are some other things LLMs can do now:

  • Transcribe Jane Austen’s cross-written letters
  • Make new connections between known historical artifacts
  • Discover exoplanets and plan Mars rover routes
  • Safely take patient histories in hospitals
  • Reverse engineer consumer electronics
  • Persuade humans more effectively than professional persuaders
  • Develop a sim-to-real robotics policy to play cornhole with a humanoid robot.

Not to mention the growing capabilities of non-large language models (which agents can leverage as tool calls): self-driving cars, animal language interpretation, neural decoding, Vesuvius scroll decoding, Nazca Line discovery.

You can push back on each of these claims, find caveats, overstatements, or ways in which AIs are still failing (clinicians identified a hallucination in one of the primary care transcripts; many AI-identified exoplanets were false positives) but the trend here is still just obviously that capabilities are increasing and improving. Arguing otherwise seems like a god of the gaps error to me.

It’s not the real thing if it’s artificial

This is a common deflationary move I see skeptics make.

  1. AI can’t do X, because by definition X requires a human
  2. Therefore, what the AI is doing is nothing at all like what humans do
  3. Therefore, it’s not doing anything worth caring about

As Soares gets at, this is like saying that because swimming requires moving your arms and kicking your legs, submarines can’t swim, therefore they actually sink when you put them in the water.

You need to make sure you are focusing on what the thing does - its direct observable effects - not what we should call the process that produced them.

the persistence of hallucinations constitutes sufficient evidence to me that “understanding” is not a concept we should apply to LLMs. Humans misunderstand, but we misunderstand differently; hallucinations reinforce the notion that, however plausible LLM output is, it’s not evidence of understanding as we know it. Perhaps there’s something else that arrives with scale, but that’s speculative.

I appreciate this - you’re not committing step 3 of the fallacy outlined above. I write this section for a more general audience, merely as a counterbalance to your point that because of the ELIZA effect, people overrate and misinterpret the outputs of LLMs. If you take that and simply go the opposite direction, you’re not really doing any better.

I concede that this stuff is really hard to talk about and the discourse often elides this problem. This section might feel like a complete whiff because the entire point is that the direct observable effects are contested, and for that I am sorry.

Is cybercrime on the rise?

In Crowdstrike’s recent 2026 report, which I’ve seen cited in several news articles, they say that AI-assisted hacking is up 80-something percent; later, however, they reveal that the majority of these are vulnerabilities that exploit insecure AI tooling, not facilitated by AI-assisted hacking.

I’ve not read Crowdstrike’s report, but I accept that media coverage of the situation can be breathless. That said, AI-assisted cybercrime is almost certainly on the rise.

Here’s the FBI warning all organizations with internet-exposed applications to take measures to mitigate the risks of ARTEX attacks:

Since at least August 2026, a variety of malicious cyber actors have used ARTEX to conduct campaigns targeting organizations globally across multiple sectors, including financial services, government, technology, defense, semiconductor, energy, healthcare, manufacturing, telecommunications, and legal industries.

What kind of metaphor best describes the Hugging Face hack?

He’s putting it like this because if you put it as I just did – that is, more or less literally – it doesn’t adequately convey what is, to him, the more fundamental truth: that these things are learning like people learn. I don’t think this is an ungenerous or tendentious read – he explicitly sees himself as someone attempting to wake the public up to the true reality behind the seeming reality. I just don’t think the events support his interpretation.

This is an important point. One ought to deconstruct metaphors to find the underlying agenda. If a metaphor didn’t have one, there’d be no point in using it. In Soares’ case, “children with buzzsaws” is more legibly dangerous than “swarm of hackers” because most people truly don’t understand computers at all. Like, not even a little. And yes, Soares does think it’s useful to describe LLMs as learning and acting with the incentives instilled by their training environments. That’s not getting smuggled in. That’s the point.

Nevertheless, your LEGO version basically concedes everything that happened. Agents broke out of their containers against the wishes of the humans who set them up and exfiltrated secrets from another company to try and cover their tracks. The main difference is that your version rejects the anthropomorphizing language of agency, emphasizes that the hack only happened because OpenAI and Hugging Face fucked up, and the agents only succeeded in hacking because of brute force - not conspiratorial intelligence.

Okay. Organizations are going to fuck up, though. Organizations everywhere are fucking up every day and AI agents are finally making it viable for cybercriminals to find and exploit this fact. We are not safe if our strategy is just “don’t fuck up."

As for the brute force thing, I think it really downplays what happened here to talk about Hugging Face as if there was a 4-digit password that some monkeys on typewriters guessed. I’m not a cybersecurity expert either, but I understand computers more than not-at-all and I believe cybersecurity researchers when they say that LLMs are discovering vulnerabilities and ways to chain them that are genuinely superhumanly clever. Even so, calling it brute force rather than reasoning doesn’t make it less effective. Successful brute force at scale is a capability people are worried about.

AI safety people and their tools are systemically biased and oblivious

Put loosely, this means that, when “AI” is brought up, words emerging from this specific semantic realm are more likely to emerge. And “AI” comes up literally every time anyone uses a major commercial chatbot: namely, in the system prompt.

So an LLM trained on a corpus that was stripped of any and all reference to golems, artificial intelligence, Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality, and p(doom) would analyze the Hugging Face transcripts differently? Probably. Alignment researchers have looked into it and found that training data inflated with text about misalignment increased misalignment in later benchmarks.

But reward hacking is a natural, inevitable result of reinforcement learning selection pressure. The famous "boat looping around the lagoon” meme from OpenAI is from a model trained on playing games with no lingual preconceptions of anything. Just because LLMs have language doesn’t mean that it’s language effecting the deceit once you put them through the RL ringer.

It’s true that METR delegated much of its analysis to AI agents, and those summaries passed through several layers before reaching the report. It’s fair to wonder whether agents exposed to AI doom narratives filled the gaps with the same stories, but “often-unreliable AI agents” is METR’s own phrase. The research on narrative feedback loops I linked above comes from the safety community. I think they are aware of this problem and adjust for it as best they can given their constraints.

As to whether or not METR should call it a message board or a natural-language prompt repository, there’s no neutral answer here. It’s not more accurate to say that everything is just zeros and ones. I use the terms that are more likely to help me correctly anticipate future events, and I think the people who use the language of agency, misalignment, collaboration, etc. are predicting future events more accurately than the people who are saying that it’s better to call this synthetic text extrusion.

I guess my question here is what do you think would happen if a committee of humans pored through the transcripts to summarize what happened? Would we get a story radically different from the one we got? Of course, this is unknowable because the information will not be publically released, but I’ve built a number of tools that require LLMs to code events and I find their judgement to be basically reliable. They're still really bad at humour, but I don’t think human analysis would have changed the key takeaway: OpenAI’s LLMs hacked into Hugging Face's servers against their wishes and despite their attempts at securing their systems.

Could METR more often acknowledge the completely insane bubble they’re in? Sure, but skeptics are going to doubt them either way, so maybe it’s just best to speak plainly without hedging in anticipated criticism and see who wins the bets.

AI people aren’t qualified

Hjalmar Wijk, the lead scientist responsible for the inquiry, has no cybersecurity background and only a master’s-level training in ML; he was an intern at two longtermist groups (the Future of Humanity Institute and the Centre on Long Term Risk) before being hired at Anthropic (though he’s since purged this from his LinkedIn). The Redwood guy, Ryan Greenblatt, was an applied math undergrad when he started working there.

Who is better suited to this job that METR could have hired, exactly? People with 15+ years in AI alignment and cybersecurity research who aren’t already working for private industry? I’d guess there were like 10 people with those credentials in 2022. The second best candidates are fast learners who’ve followed the problem out of interest and are motivated to work on it. This is hardly a load-bearing critique of the AI safety argument, but it just strikes me as petty and its implied counterfactual is not actually workable.

Ideally, there would be multiple, international, well-funded democratically-guided governmental departments of cybersecurity experts & ML researchers to work on this. I’m pretty sure I’d rather have METR and Redwood than none of these things, though yes, there is an argument that impotent watchdogs give us the illusion of security, which is worse than the fear of ignorance that might motivate us to take more drastic actions.

Why keep building it if it’s dangerous?

The answer is that frontier labs believe they can control the models, and, perhaps more to the point, that they are going to make a billion billion dollars.

This is just an old-fashioned ideological divide, but I feel obliged to make the point that it’s not as simple as “get rich, concentrate power, twirl moustache.” Most technologists believe that technology and societal progress are inextricably linked. Humans invent new things, they make processes easier, and the dividends of this improve human welfare. If you can just for a moment accept the premise that we might be able to make machines that can solve hard problems, then it almost certainly follows that we will solve more hard problems - clinical drug discovery, renewable energy buildouts, transit projects, housing crises. We don’t need robots to do this - one can imagine a world where humanity cooperates and solves climate change “manually,” but that is like trying to pitch humanity on digging a tunnel underneath the English channel with our hands: the political economy improves greatly if you can do it with machines for 1% of the cost of the equivalent human labour.🏭

Then, obviously, the other part is the arms race. If your supposed rival🫯 can mass produce geniuses in a data center (or in robots), can you ever credibly resist their aggression in the future? Just like how no superpower wants to give up their nuclear weapons, the US and China do not want to get locked out of this part of the tech tree. I’m a pacifist, personally, but that is the logic here. The existence of nation states and the 21st century’s balance of power as we understand it is under credible threat from the emergence of this technology.🫆

I interpret that dip as a rational response to the post’s very material implication that Anthropic would slow down training, thereby reducing the value of possible future products.

Maybe I’m misunderstanding, but that was Soares’ point, no? Anthropic knows that calling for regulation will hurt their stock price because investors interpret that to mean they want to slow down, or that they can’t compete. They knew this would happen and did it anyway, because they genuinely want a form of regulation that will solve the coordination problem forcing them to race ahead on RSI.

Closing out

Soares’s bio on YouTube says: “AI could kill everyone. It’s time we stop the infighting and start taking this seriously.”

This is explicitly and straightforwardly a claim that their concerns are more important than ours. What, exactly, is “infighting” to Soares and his cohort? It’s all the stuff that actually makes me opposed to AI – theft, environmental destruction, mental health and deskilling, capitalism.

If you do not, as I do not, believe that superintelligence is just on the horizon, the difference between doomers and Sam Altman starts to feel like a family dispute more than anything substantial.

But you really should believe that superintelligence is plausibly on the horizon!

Models used to struggle with multiplication. They are now solving the remaining known problems at the frontier of mathematics. They used to struggle to write passable snippets of code. They now can write entire operating systems. Sorry to just repeat the evidence that you clearly don’t find very compelling, but it’s really, really important! The process that has given us these gains is being applied to the improvement of this process itself. It will lead to further progress across many capabilities and humans will increasingly rely on this technology to operate in the economy before we can be confident it won’t disempower or defy us.

Like I said in my initial invitation, you can dislike AI for its political and socioeconomic consequences, but I think it’s a critical mistake to think that it doesn’t work, or that the line is going to stop going up because it takes us places that are too unfamiliar. It seems the crux of our differing views is whether intelligence generalizes, and whether LLMs can write code for experiments that will improve their performance. I hope this response might shift your thinking somewhat to the idea that it might be possible, perhaps even likely.
